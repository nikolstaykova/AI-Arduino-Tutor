Final-year project — research brief · rev. 2026-09-20

Pinpoint

A platform that teaches real, hands-on skills by watching the learner through a camera and correcting them live — from a human specialist, or from an AI tutor that must first learn to see the workbench as precisely as a person does.

Domain Arduino & electrical circuits Status concept validated → pre-build Core question can geometry + an LLM find the exact hole?
01

How we landed on this domain

The idea didn't start here. It moved through three re-scopes before settling — each one cut by checking what already exists, not by preference.

Starting point

Remote-expert AR glasses

A specialist guides a field user live through AR glasses. Real, but already a mature B2B market — RealWear, Vuzix, TeamViewer Frontline, Microsoft Dynamics 365 Remote Assist all do this for industrial field service today.

Pivot

Consumer skill-learning, not enterprise service

Reframed as "Duolingo, but for a real hands-on skill" — same live-guidance mechanic, aimed at an individual learner rather than a company's technician.

Considered & rejected

Appliance / device repair

Checked the market: iFixit's FixBot, plus several photo-diagnosis apps, already own this space with a trusted incumbent brand. Crowded, not where the open ground is.

Committed

Arduino & electrical circuits

Real hardware, an observable pass/fail state, an existing maker community to draw curriculum from — and, critically, a geometry that's actually solvable: a breadboard is a fixed, known grid.

03

The gap, precisely

The unsolved piece is narrow and specific: telling a learner which exact breadboard hole or Arduino pin something belongs in — from a camera image, live. Not "is there a fault" — the exact cell. Two independent findings confirm this is still open, not just unattempted.

SmartBreadboard-3D attempts exactly this — its own README lists the hole-detection module as an unbuilt stub ("Phase 9+"). GitHub · closest known prior art, unfinished "The Spatial Blindspot of Vision-Language Models" — general-purpose multimodal models, the Claude/GPT-4V/Gemini class included, are documented to be unreliable at fine-grained spatial localization without help. arXiv 2601.09954
04

What we're building

A live camera-guided tutor for one board (Arduino Uno) and a first lesson track in basic circuits, priced in two tiers: a human specialist, or the AI tutor described below. Split cleanly by what's solved engineering versus what's the actual research.

Build — existing tools

Engineering, not research

  • Marker & corner detection — OpenCV's built-in ArUco tracking
  • Homography / grid mapping — standard OpenCV geometry
  • The reasoning layer — Claude via API, used as-is
  • Quick-question chat — same API, given the live lesson state
  • Specialist ⇄ learner channel — TLS / WebRTC's built-in encryption, never hand-rolled
  • Mistake & accuracy logging — plain application analytics
  • Lesson design & ground truth — define each lesson's correct circuit as a Wokwi diagram.json (explicit pin-to-pin connections, machine-readable), auto-validated by Wokwi CI's headless simulation before it's ever used as an answer key
  • Voice interaction — off-the-shelf text-to-speech for the tutor's spoken directions, speech-to-text for the learner's answers ("yes I know this tool" / "no, show me")
  • Readiness check & fallback tutorials — short pre-recorded clips for common tools/skills, played on demand when the learner says they don't know one
Research — the contribution

Grid-augmented spatial grounding

  • Central question: can a general-purpose LLM, combined with our geometric preprocessing, reliably perform hole-level verification of breadboard wiring — and how does that compare against classical CV / a dedicated vision model built specifically for this task?
  • Either result is a real finding: the LLM approach earns its place, or a purpose-built classical detector is simpler and just as good
  • Nobody has shipped it for this domain — confirmed against the nearest prior art
  • Doubles as the dissertation's contribution and the product's core differentiator
Doesn't compete

Wokwi already checks whether a virtual circuit built inside its own software is wired correctly — and its diagram.json format plus headless wokwi-cli CI runner mean lesson answer-keys can be authored and auto-validated as structured data, not screenshots. It never watches a real physical board through a camera, though — that's still the unsolved half of the problem this project is actually about.

Division of labor

Wokwi's job: define and pre-validate the correct circuit, as a hole-level answer key — its virtual breadboard already names every hole (e.g. bb1:18t.d = row 18, top section, column d). Our research's job, unchanged: turn a real photo into that same hole-name format. Once both sides agree on naming, checking correctness is a plain structural comparison — no further research needed for that last step.

System flow, start to finish

Everything above, in the order it actually happens — before a lesson exists, and while a learner is doing it.

  1. 1

    Author (before the lesson exists)

    Free Wokwi account, build the lesson circuit in the ordinary editor — e.g. "wire an LED to pin 13." The diagram.json exists the moment it's built, no paid plan needed.

  2. 2

    Validate

    Run it through Wokwi CI to auto-confirm it actually behaves correctly (the LED really blinks) before trusting it as ground truth.

  3. 3

    Ship the answer key

    That diagram.json is the lesson's hole-level correct answer, done once, offline, by whoever designs the lesson.

  4. 4

    Learner starts the step (wide shot)

    The tutor speaks first: what's needed for this step — "you'll need a small breadboard and a 220Ω resistor" — and identifies/circles those items wherever they sit on the cluttered table.

  5. 5

    Readiness check

    Tutor asks, out loud: "do you have this, and do you know how to use it?" Waits for a spoken answer — no rushing ahead.

  6. 6

    Fallback micro-tutorial, if needed

    Learner says no → a short predefined video for that specific tool/skill plays, then the tutor checks back in before continuing.

  7. 7

    Guided build (close-up)

    Learner brings the gathered items in close; spoken, paced directions — "now connect the LED to pin 13" — plus a highlight drawn at the exact known pin location. The tutor waits for the learner to actually act.

  8. 8

    Checkpoint verification

    Triggered by the learner saying so — "I'm done" / "check it" — not an inferred signal. Our geometry model maps the camera view onto the same hole-naming scheme, then a plain logic check compares the detected hole-names against the Wokwi answer key — no LLM in the comparison itself, just a deterministic match/no-match.

  9. 9

    Feedback

    Matches → confirm and move on, no LLM needed. Only on a mismatch does the LLM get called in — not to re-check the same thing, but to work out what went wrong and explain it usefully ("looks like you're one row off — move it to hole 13").

05

How it might work

The exact mechanism is the research question, not a settled fact — this is the current working idea, in plain terms, before any of it is tested.

01SeeCapture what the learner's camera is looking at
02Identify the boardRecognize which model it is — v1 targets one: the Arduino Uno
03Map the pointsWork out where every hole/pin actually is — the hard, unsolved part
04Guide & catch errorsPoint to where things go, flag what's wrong
Leading idea

For step 3 — the actual unknown — the strongest candidate so far: use fixed reference points on the board to work out a grid geometrically, then let the AI model read that labeled grid instead of guessing raw coordinates from the photo. Backed by published findings that raw AI vision alone struggles at exact spatial localization (Spatial Blindspot of VLMs) but improves a lot once given that kind of visual scaffolding (Grid-augmented vision). Still needs to be built and tested against real breadboard photos before it's more than a hypothesis — see the research plan below.

Three different capabilities — only one is the hard research

Step ④ above actually splits into two very different jobs, and there's an earlier one worth naming too.

1
Recognition, not precision

Identify loose objects

"Which one's the resistor, roughly where is it on the table." VLMs are actually fine here — the spatial blind spot is about exact coordinates, not "what is this thing." Well-trodden ground already. Doesn't need the novel research.

2
Pure geometry

Point to a known board location

"Here's output 3." Once the board is tracked, its pin layout is fixed and documented — transform a known coordinate into the live view and draw a highlight. No AI call needed at all.

3
The actual research

Verify the actual connection

"Did the wire really end up there." Detecting something new and unknown, not a fixed reference — the hard, unsolved precision-grounding problem.

This implies a natural two-phase UX: a wide shot where the system circles what's needed for the step (capability 1), then a close-up once the learner gathers those items to connect them, where pointing and verifying (2 and 3) take over. The wide→close transition doubles as the mode-switch signal.

06

Research plan, once the system exists

The build proves the pipeline runs. This is what proves it's right — the part a dissertation actually has to defend.

  1. 1

    Ground-truth test set

    Real breadboard photos, wiring known and labeled hole-by-hole, shot across lighting and angle conditions.

  2. 2

    Grid-render tuning

    Vary line weight, transparency, and labeling scheme; find what the LLM actually reads best.

  3. 3

    Prompt / context design

    Test how much domain documentation — breadboard conventions, the lesson's expected circuit — actually improves accuracy.

  4. 4

    Baseline comparison — the core of the thesis

    Raw LLM (no grid) vs. grid-augmented LLM vs. classical CV / a dedicated vision model built specifically for this task (no LLM at all). Not a formality — this is the actual research question: does the LLM approach earn its place, or does a purpose-built classical detector do just as well or better?

  5. 5

    Accuracy measurement

    Hole-identification accuracy, false-positive/negative wiring calls, end-to-end latency.

  6. 6

    Failure analysis

    Where does it actually break — glare, wire color, occlusion, off-axis angle — and why.

  7. 7

    Lock the scope boundary

    Decide, from the data, exactly how much lesson complexity the accuracy can support for v1.

07

Multiple models, timing & where it runs

Three practical questions that shape the build, answered by patterns already proven elsewhere rather than invented from scratch.

Q · multiple models?

Route by skill, not just by cost

Research: yes — test Claude, GPT-class, and Gemini against the same grid-overlay set, but per sub-skill (reading the grid labels, reasoning about expected wiring, writing feedback), not just one overall score.

Production, two different levers: task-based routing — send each kind of question to whichever model is actually best at it, independent of price; and separately, cost-cascading — a cheap fast model handles routine checks, escalating to a pricier one only when unsure. On low confidence, the fallback is "ask the model that's good at this," not just "ask a bigger one" — human is the last resort if nothing's confident.

→ the AI tier's uncertainty threshold becomes the natural trigger for "want a human specialist instead?"
Q · used constantly?

Two speeds, two schedules

Geometry/tracking (finding the board, keeping the grid aligned) is cheap classical math — runs continuously, every frame, like any AR overlay.

The comparison itself ("is this wired right?") is a plain logic check — detected hole-names vs. the Wokwi answer key, deterministic, no LLM, effectively free.

The LLM only fires at checkpoints, primarily the learner saying so ("I'm done" / "check it") — and even then, only if the logic check found a mismatch. A correct answer never needs an LLM call; its job is explaining what went wrong, not deciding whether something's wrong.

Q · local or server?

Hybrid — split by speed need

On-device: geometry/tracking/overlay rendering. Lag past ~100ms reads as visibly broken, tighter than any network round trip.

Cloud API: the multimodal LLM call itself — a frontier model isn't something a phone runs, and since it's occasional (not per-frame), a 1–3s round trip is fine.

→ same split Apple Intelligence and Gemini Nano already use in production
Net decision: local, continuous geometric tracking + grid rendering on-device (OpenCV) — cloud, checkpoint-triggered multimodal calls, routed cheap-first with escalation to a stronger model or a human when confidence is low. Still open: the exact confidence threshold for escalation, and which specific models pair well for the cascade — both wait on the benchmarking results from the research plan above.
08

Generalizability & where to stop

Transfers

Any flat, gridded, standardized layout works the same way — a car fuse box or relay panel is nearly the same geometry problem as a breadboard. Worth a line in future work, not a reason to widen scope now.

Doesn't transfer

A 3D engine bay has no single flat plane and no fixed layout across car models — a different problem (semantic part recognition), and one where ARI, MECH AI, Fixomotive and Identifix already compete hard. Breadboard/Arduino stays the right first domain because it's the one place this exact technique is uncontested.
